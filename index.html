FRANKLIN MOUNTAINS STATE PARK · TRAIL USE STUDY

Who's on the trail, and where do they go?

Two and a half years of anonymized device-location data from the trail network inside El Paso's Franklin Mountains State Park — mapped, timed, and traced from trailhead to summit.

JAN 2021 – MAY 2023  ·  — DEVICES  ·  — TRAIL VISITS

ABOUT THIS DATA

Franklin Mountains State Park is a 26,000-acre wedge of Chihuahuan desert running straight through the middle of El Paso — more than 100 named trail segments threading between Tom Mays, McKelligon Canyon, and the peaks above the city. This study is built from four exports of hashed, anonymized mobile-location data covering the park's trail polygons: a raw pathing feed (every ping a device produces while inside a trail segment), a visit-pin log, a home-location panel, and a per-trail visitor count. No device is ever identified — the hashed IDs let us trace one anonymous visitor's movement between trails without knowing who they are.

We stitched consecutive pings from the same device into trail visits (a new visit starts after a 4‑hour gap in activity) to answer a simple set of questions: which trails do people actually walk, what do they do once they're past the trailhead, where in the park does foot traffic cluster hardest, where do visitors live, and when — by season, day, and hour — does the park fill up.

        Notes on the data

        All figures are built from four hashed-device-ID exports covering Franklin Mountains State Park's trail polygons, January 2021 through May 2023. Trail "visits" are reconstructed by grouping consecutive pings from the same device with fewer than 4 hours between them; a visit's first touched trail is treated as its entry point. Hotspot cells use a ~30 meter grid on the raw pathing feed and count distinct devices, not raw pings, so a single idling phone can't manufacture a hotspot. Home-location figures are aggregated to protect individual privacy — no single household is ever identifiable on the map or in the ZIP table, and cells with fewer than 3 devices are dropped entirely.

        This is passive mobile-location panel data, not a park attendance census: it reflects wherever the data provider's device panel had coverage, which can shift over the 2.5-year window independent of real-world visitation. Treat year-over-year comparisons as directional, not exact.

        Trail geometry and the park boundary are published by the City of El Paso GIS open data portal. Code and data pipeline: github.com/hoffmanap.
